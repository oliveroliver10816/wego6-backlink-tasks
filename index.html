## Why the chemist is the first instinct

British shoppers have been trained by decades of high-street pharmacy to check a familiar shelf first, and for most everyday things that instinct is exactly right. You know the aisle, you can pick the box up, and you can read the panel on the back before deciding anything.

Supplements have partly moved away from that model. A good number are now issued directly by the company behind them and sold from a single website rather than distributed to retailers. That is not a warning sign in itself; it changes where you should look rather than whether you should look at all.

## The two-minute way to settle a stock question

Three checks, in this order. Start with the retailer's own website search rather than a general search engine, because only the retailer's system knows its current range. Most large chains also put a branch stock checker on the product page, which is the closest thing to a definitive answer a screen will give you. If a product does not appear in that system at all, you have your answer for that retailer.

Second, ring your nearest branch. It takes a minute, and staff will tell you plainly what is on the system. Third, check whether the maker sells direct. Almost every supplement brand states its own route to purchase somewhere on its site, and if a brand sells only from its own address, no shop search will ever produce a result however many times you rephrase it.

## Why the maker's own listing is the useful place to land

When a product is issued directly, the label detail, the pack structure and the terms of sale all sit in one place instead of being summarised second-hand by a dozen other pages of unknown age. That is the practical argument for going [straight to the source that issues the product](https://buy-wego6.com/uk/boots/) instead of assembling an answer out of search snippets, forum threads and screenshots.

It also removes a real hazard of buying online. Where a listing has been copied — photography lifted, the name altered by a character or two — the copy always lives somewhere other than the brand's own address, so landing on that address takes the whole category of problem off the table.

## What to read on the label before you order

WEGO 6 is a supplement in capsule form. Each capsule declares 387 mg, the intake is one capsule a day, and a pack holds thirty capsules, which is what makes one pack equal one month. The capsule shell is vegan.

The maker names apple cider vinegar and zinc specifically, and describes the rest as plant-based ingredients and amino acids. The full list is on the pack. That individual naming is what I look for on any product. A formula described only as a "proprietary blend", with no quantity attached to anything in it, cannot be assessed by anybody — including by the people writing about it.

## Buying without a counter

The counter does two things you lose online: it hands you the box to read, and it gives you somebody to ask. Both are replaceable if you are deliberate. Read the ingredient panel on screen before ordering rather than after. Decide honestly whether a daily capsule fits your day, because a routine you cannot picture yourself keeping is the usual reason a pack ends up in a drawer. Keep the order confirmation email in a folder, since every later question refers back to it.

If you are pregnant, breastfeeding, taking regular medication or sensitive to caffeine, ask your GP before starting any supplement. That is the one part of the high-street experience genuinely worth recreating.

Meine Erfahrung: Die Variante, die auf den ersten Blick „die vernünftige“ ist, deckt sich selten mit der Kurdauer, die ich mir eigentlich vorgenommen hatte.

Vier Punkte, die dabei regelmäßig untergehen:

**Portion ist nicht gleich Kapsel.** Alle Mengenangaben auf dem Etikett beziehen sich auf die empfohlene Tagesportion. Besteht die aus zwei oder drei Kapseln, liegt der Gehalt je Kapsel entsprechend niedriger. Einmal umrechnen, dann ist das Bild klar.

Zweitens die Chargennummer notieren oder die Verpackung einmal abfotografieren — bei einer Rückfrage an den Hersteller ist das die erste Angabe, nach der gefragt wird.

Große Wissenschaft ist das alles nicht. Es verhindert aber die zwei häufigsten Fehlkäufe: zu viel für eine Kur, die man ohnehin nicht durchhält, und zu wenig für eine, die man ernst gemeint hatte.

# What to Check Before Ordering a Supplement Online

*By Gareth Iveson*

Almost everything you need in order to judge an online supplement order is already on the page in front of you. The trouble is that it is scattered — part in the description, part in the label image, part in the small print and part in the terms — so hardly anyone reads all of it in one sitting. I have started doing exactly that, and it takes about five minutes.

When something looks worth trying, I open the order page and [hold it to that standard before confirming anything](https://buy-wego6.com/uk/buy/), rather than reading the description and taking the rest on trust. Four things I look for:

**Who you are buying from.** Supplements sold in the UK are regulated as food, and a named business has to be responsible for the product, with a contactable address. On a website that should translate into a company name, a proper postal address and a way of reaching someone. A contact form with no name behind it is worth noticing.

**What the product is.** The full ingredient list in descending order of weight, the amount of each active per stated daily dose, allergen information, and how many capsules a pack contains. If the daily dose is two or three capsules, divide before you choose a size — a pack count on its own tells you nothing about how long it lasts.

**What you are agreeing to.** Whether the order is a one-off or a recurring arrangement, and if it recurs, how long it runs and how it is stopped. Distance selling rules give cancellation rights on most online orders, but there are exceptions, so read the seller's own returns and cancellation terms rather than assuming what they say.

**Whether anyone answers.** Send one specific question — about the dose, the ingredient form, or the pack — and see what comes back. A clear reply from a person is a real signal; a template that ignores the question is one too.

It is surprising how often one of those four is not addressed anywhere on the page, and that absence is itself information.

Two things afterwards. Keep the confirmation email and the order reference; if a query ever comes up, that is the first thing you will be asked for. And when the pack arrives, take the batch code and the best-before date from the label itself rather than from the listing.

One last point no web page can settle for you: if you take prescription medicines or have an ongoing condition, run the ingredient list past a pharmacist or your GP before you start. They will read a label far faster than you can.

# Why Plenty of Supplements Never Reach the High Street

*By Ian Vosper*

Walk into a chemist and the supplement aisle looks like a summary of the market. It isn't. It is a summary of what one buying team decided to range, and those are very different things.

Shelf space is the scarcest resource in retail. A national chain plans its ranges months ahead, allocates a fixed number of facings to each category, and reviews them on a fixed cycle. Getting into that space usually means clearing volume commitments, packaging and barcode requirements, distribution through a wholesaler and, often, a contribution towards in-store promotions. Staying there means selling at a rate a category manager can defend against everything else queueing for the same few centimetres. Plenty of ordinary, unremarkable products never clear that bar, because they are new, niche, or sold in a format the chain does not carry.

There is also traffic in the other direction: brands that never apply. Selling directly keeps control of the description, the packaging and the customer relationship, and it avoids splitting one margin between two businesses for the same product. For a small operation that arithmetic often does not work, so it stays online deliberately. Most of us then default to a name we already know — Boots, a supermarket aisle, whatever is on the way home — and read absence as a verdict. It usually isn't one.

What a missing listing genuinely tells you is fairly limited: that a commercial agreement does not exist, or has not been sought. It says nothing about what is in the capsule, who made it, or to what standard.

What does tell you something is the paperwork. In the UK, food supplements are regulated as food, and the same rules follow the product wherever it is sold: a named business responsible for it with a contactable address, a full ingredient declaration in descending order of weight, allergen information, a batch code, a best-before date and a stated daily dose. The sensible approach is to [apply the same checks wherever you happen to be buying](https://buy-wego6.com/uk/boots/), rather than letting the setting do your thinking for you. Ask who is behind it, what a serving actually contains, and whether anyone answers when you write to them.

How to Read a Supplement Label Properly

# How to Read a Supplement Label Properly

*By Nadia Crowther*

The front of a supplement box is written by a marketing department. The back is written by regulation. If you want to know what you are actually buying, turn it over.

Start with the serving size: everything else on the panel hangs off it. A label often declares its figures per serving rather than per capsule, and a serving is frequently two capsules, sometimes three. Get that wrong and you can misread a product by a factor of two before looking at a single ingredient. Check the number of servings per pack too — that, not the capsule count, tells you how long one lasts.

Then the units. Milligrams and micrograms are separated by a thousand-fold and sit side by side on the same panel: vitamin D and B12 are usually declared in micrograms, most minerals and botanicals in milligrams. A glance at "400" means nothing until you read the two letters after it.

Percentages only appear where there is something to compare against. Vitamins and minerals have Nutrient Reference Values, so you get a %NRV column; plant extracts have none, so they appear as a plain weight. A missing percentage is not a gap in the label; no reference figure exists for that ingredient.

The ingredient list runs by weight, heaviest first. Anything near the end is there in a small quantity, whatever the front of the pack highlights. Bulking agents, anti-caking agents and the capsule shell all take up room there, which is normal — though the shell is worth reading if you avoid gelatine.

Extracts need a second look. A "10:1 extract" is concentrated, so 100mg of it is not comparable with 100mg of plain milled powder. Good labels state the ratio and what the extract is equivalent to; where several ingredients are grouped under one combined total, you cannot tell how it is split.

None of this takes long once you know the order to read it in, and it is the one part of the box that must be specific. Before buying anything, it is worth learning to [check the per-serving figures rather than the headline on the front](https://buy-wego6.com/uk/), because the gap between those two numbers is usually the whole story.

The label also carries the statutory lines: a recommended daily dose, an instruction not to exceed it, and a reminder that supplements are no substitute for a varied and balanced diet. Easy to skim past, and the most honest sentences on the box.
